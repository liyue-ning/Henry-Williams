EVALUATION

SIGNIFICANCE
I believe that Henry Williams was one of the most significant and impactful figures in the early spreading of the Gospel in Aotearoa. What stood out most to me was how much more impactful and thoughtful Williams' approach to spreading the Gospel was compared to Marsden's earlier approach where he believed that it was necessary to civilise the Māori; teaching them trade, farming, and other European skills Marsden believed it was crucial in preparation for Christianity and learning the Gospel. Personally, I don't think this is a good approach. Anybody, regardless of background, ethnicity or status, is free to learn about the Gospel and know of God; Christianity was never to explicitly be a 'European thing'. Before Williams arrived at Paiaha in 1823, Marsden's method won very few converts and likely even lost a few Māori's interest. When Williams arrived, he put his comfort aside, making his first course of action to put the Gospel before starting anything else. Practical work like building schools, advanced agricultural practices, and literacy came after and only supported the message further, never becoming it. I think this was the first most significant turning point created by Williams as it allowed the Māori to learn about and accept the Gospel as they were without having to conform to Williams' own European culture. Functionally, he also organised the mission better, built on the discipline of his navy background. Williams even risked his life as a peace maker, for example: when he voluntarily acted as a mediator between Ngāpuhi and Te Arawa in Tauranga in 1830, allowing missionaries to travel easier and with less tribal barriers. Essentially, this made me realise how peace acted as the soil the Gospel grew in, especially in Aotearoa. Samuel Marsden had brought the Gospel to Aotearoa, and Henry Williams spread it as he wasthe one who devoted the rest of his life to maintaining the spread of the Gospel in Aotearoa. It made the CMS (Church Missionary Society) missionary's responsibility of spreading the Gospel much more efficient and easier with Williams as their organiser/leader instead.

MISTRANSLATIONS
However, the same translations Williams had made were the same strength that became a major weakness, turning into a significant problem in the translation of the Treaty of Waitangi into Te reo. On the 4th-5th of February 1840, Williams and his son, Edward Williams, translated the Treaty of Waitangi into Te Reo in only a few hours. The most failed piece of translation that impacted the Maori significantly was how the English text said Māori gave up “sovereignty”, but the Māori text used kāwanatanga, closer to “governorship”, while also promising tino rangatiratanga, full chieftainship over lands and treasures. Many Māori chiefs also believed that the English translation was keeping much more power than the translation had said. This was one of the biggest failures of Williams as it had also displaced some of the trust the Māori's had in the missionaries and CMS; not made on purpose. Many factors contributed to this, especially the deadline set by European authorities of the translation of the treaty that was forced to be fully done overnight to be signed the next morning. I believe that the mistranslations were not at the fault of Williams' conscience since he was given such a major task to be finished in such a short amount of time. To be honest, with my limited amount of knowledge as of now, I would also shift a small part of the blame directly to the Māori chiefs and rangitara as well. Although Williams was fluent in Te Reo after 17 years of working with the CMS in Aotearoa, he was still native in English and such a significantly impactful piece of translation should most definitely be translation-checked by native Māori speakers before it was finalised; technical words such as 'sovereignty' could be easily mistranslated into a more general understanding. However, the time control was still the main factor that negatively impacted most of the mistranslations, which I solely blame on the European authorities, not Williams or Māori.

OVERALL SIGNIFICANCE AND FALLOUT
Overall, I believe Henry Williams sincerely cared and did his best for Māori even if he wasn't perfect in the end. He had risked both his life and European trust/status in an effort of peacemaking because he was seen taking the Māori's side in battles between the Europeans and it shows me that he was willing to pay a price for his beliefs. His 40 years of living in Aotearoa unfortunately ended with him making large land purchases for his family, leading the CMS to dismiss him. I find it both sad and reflective how his eventual imperfection reinforces my realisation that human greed eventually always overtakes their compassion as it's just a part of humanity that we can't escape. The mistranslations in the signed treaty also still impact life in Aotearoa today with many political complications, advocacy, and the continued fight for Māori rights and tradition. In the end, I view Williams as a good man with a God-guided path who was still human with real blind-spots. His significance on the early spread of the Gospel in Aotearoa teaches me that judging someone by result isn't as accurate as judging someone by their method or having a timely understanding of how the result came to be.
